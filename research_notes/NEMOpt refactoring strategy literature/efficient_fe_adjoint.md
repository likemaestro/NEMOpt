## Q1. Sparse assembly: triplet/COO vs incremental CSC insertion, preallocated pattern with in-place reassembly, assembling one triangle

### Takeaway
Every source agrees that incremental insertion into an empty compressed sparse column (CSC) matrix is the slowest option. Building from triplets (COO) and compressing once is the classic fix: top88 vectorized the triplet build, and fsparse made the compression step faster. The fastest pattern for repeated reassembly with a fixed connectivity is to compute the sparsity pattern once and then add values in place into the preallocated CSC (Ferrite-style assembler). Assembling only one triangle and wrapping it as `Symmetric` roughly halves assembly time and memory. It also makes the matrix exactly symmetric by construction, which removes the 1e-14 asymmetry that currently pushes Julia's `\` onto LU.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR] Andreassen, Clausen, Schevenels, Lazarov & Sigmund (2011)**, "Efficient topology optimization in MATLAB using 88 lines of code", *Struct. Multidisc.   - The speedup comes "mainly by preallocating arrays and vectorizing loops". The paper reports "a factor of 100 speed improvement" for the 150×50 MBB mesh: 75.19 s vs 0.72 s per iteration with sensitivity filtering, measured on a Core2 Duo U9400 with MATLAB R2010a. [Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946)
  - Assembly builds triplets `iK, jK, sK` from `edofMat` with a Kronecker product and compresses once with `sparse`, where "specifying the same row and column indices multiple times results in a summation". The paper says this "could be further improved by using the sparse2 function from CHOLMOD … faster … due to … more efficient sorting algorithm". [Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946)
  - **Directly relevant to NEMOpt's 1e-14 asymmetry:** the code symmetrizes `K = (K+K')/2` because symmetry "determines the algorithm used by MATLAB … If the stiffness matrix is … symmetric, and has real positive diagonal elements, Cholesky factorization is used. If the stiffness matrix is not symmetric (due to rounding errors in the assembly procedure), LU factorization is used instead, resulting in a longer computation time." [Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946)
- **[PR] Ferrari & Sigmund (2020)**, "A new generation 99 line Matlab code for compliance topology optimization and its extension to 3D", *Struct. Multidisc. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05436)

  - "fsparse gives a speedup of 170–250% compared to sparse2" on a single core. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05436)
  - Overall gains per iteration are top99neo vs top88 "2.66, 3.85 and 5.5 times" (300×100, 600×200, 1200×400) and 1.55/1.57/1.78× vs top88U, which already uses sparse2 and conv2. - **[DOC] Julia SparseArrays**: `sparse(I,J,V)` sums duplicates (`combine` defaults to `+`), and "numerical zeros … are retained as structural nonzeros". [Julia docs](https://docs.julialang.org/en/v1/stdlib/SparseArrays/)

### Inferences
- NEMOpt's measured 4.6 s (incremental) vs 1.4 s (triplet) at 80×80 matches the literature ranking. The Ferrite numbers suggest that a precomputed pattern with in-place `addindex!`-style accumulation would cut the remaining 1.4 s by a further large factor. The pattern is fixed for the whole optimization: fixed mesh, fixed periodic DOF map. The per-design cost then reduces to zeroing `nzval` and accumulating into it.
- For the reduced (periodic) system, build the pattern directly in reduced DOF numbering (see Q3). Assemble only the upper (or lower) triangle and wrap it in `Symmetric(K, :U)`. This kills the 1e-14 asymmetry at the source and halves memory and assembly work, which matches Ferrari & Sigmund's ~50%. The alternative `(K+K')/2` à la top88 costs about as much as one-triangle assembly (Ferrari & Sigmund) but keeps the full storage.
- Element matrices from a hand-coded or AD-generated neo-Hookean tangent can be asymmetric at round-off level. One-triangle assembly makes the global matrix exactly symmetric regardless.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed benchmark in Julia comparing `sparse(I,J,V)` vs a preallocated in-place assembler for a 2D quad mesh of NEMOpt size. The Ferrite numbers are for a heat equation on an M3; NEMOpt should micro-benchmark its own case.
- I did not check what sorting algorithm Julia's `sparse` uses internally (counting sort vs comparison sort), so the fsparse-vs-MATLAB speedups do not transfer directly.

## Q2. Linear solvers in TO: factorization reuse for multiple RHS, Cholesky vs LDLᵀ vs LU, CHOLMOD/UMFPACK, iterative and multigrid alternatives

### Takeaway
At NEMOpt's size (2D, roughly 3·10³–1.3·10⁴ DOFs), a sparse direct Cholesky (CHOLMOD) of the exactly symmetric reduced tangent is the right tool. Factor once per distinct matrix and back-substitute for all right-hand sides: the 4 χ cases plus the adjoint(s). Reuse the symbolic analysis across Newton iterations and designs with `cholesky!(F, K)`. Use LDLᵀ, or Cholesky with a fallback, only when indefiniteness can occur. Multigrid-preconditioned CG and PETSc-style parallel frameworks pay off only at around 10⁵–10⁶ DOFs or more, or in 3D.

### Inferences
- The coordinator's measurement (explicit Cholesky with 4 RHS: 0.08 s vs 0.61 s) is consistent with top88's warning and with the dispatch logic above. Two changes realize the gain in production code. First, make the reduced tangent exactly symmetric (Q1). Second, call `cholesky(Symmetric(K))` explicitly, not `\`, keep the `Factor`, and reuse it for every solve with the same matrix.
- Factorization budget per design evaluation, compared with the current 59:
  - (a) The filter matrix is constant, so factor it **once per optimization run** (Q6), giving 0 per design.
  - (b) Newton: one numeric factorization per Newton iteration. The symbolic analysis is reused via `cholesky!(F, K)`, because the pattern never changes.
  - (c) One factorization of the tangent at the converged state, shared by the 4 χ solves (one multi-column back-substitution) and all adjoint solves (Q4). If the convergence check is placed after assembling K at the new iterate, that last factor is already the converged-state tangent and can be reused directly.
  - The non-Newton factorizations therefore shrink from "4 χ + n_adjoint + filter" to 1.
- Indefiniteness: a compressible neo-Hookean tangent at a stable equilibrium is expected to be positive definite. Low-density, distorted elements or buckled microstructures can make it indefinite, as Senne et al. assume for geometric nonlinearity. Recommended pattern: `F = cholesky(Symmetric(K); check=false)`, and if `!issuccess(F)` fall back to `ldlt` (or LU). Log how often the fallback triggers, because a non-PD converged tangent has physical meaning (instability).
- Iterative or multigrid solvers, and factorization-reuse reanalysis (Amir & Sigmund; Senne et al.), are approximations that change the numbers. For a behavior-preserving refactor they should be optional, off by default, and validated against the direct solver.

### Gaps
- I did not verify in the CHOLMOD user guide whether CHOLMOD's LDLᵀ performs numerical pivoting; I believe it does not, which would mean possible instability for strongly indefinite matrices [UNVERIFIED]. Check before relying on `ldlt` for indefinite tangents.
- I found no study benchmarking Cholesky vs LDLᵀ vs LU specifically for periodic hyperelastic unit-cell tangents.

## Q3. Homogenization-specific efficiency: energy-based homogenization, periodic BC implementations (master–slave elimination vs Lagrange multipliers vs penalty), multiple load cases with one factorization

### Takeaway
Educational homogenization TO codes enforce periodicity strongly: they identify slave DOFs with master DOFs in the DOF numbering and remove the rigid-body nullspace by fixing one node, or by nullspace removal in Firedrake. The unit-strain load cases are then solved against one reduced SPD matrix. Strong elimination keeps the system small and SPD. Lagrange multipliers make it an indefinite saddle-point system, and penalty methods trade accuracy against conditioning. For NEMOpt, assembling directly in reduced numbering gives an exactly symmetric reduced matrix, avoids forming Tᵀ K T, and lets the 4 χ problems share one factorization.

  - "Affine constraints will impact the sparsity pattern of the matrix", so the docs recommend `allocate_matrix(dh, ch)`. For Newton, `apply_zero!` handles residuals and increments. [Ferrite docs](https://ferrite-fem.github.io/Ferrite.jl/stable/topics/constraints/)

### Inferences
- NEMOpt's master–slave elimination is literature-consistent; it is the "strong" approach used by Thillaithevan et al. and by the 88-line-derived codes. To make it efficient, avoid forming Tᵀ K T in floating point, a likely source of the 1e-14 asymmetry. Instead, map each element's DOFs through the master–slave map before scatter-add, so the element contribution lands directly in reduced numbering. Since T is a 0/1 selection matrix for periodic tying, this gives exactly Tᵀ K T in exact arithmetic, with no sparse matrix-matrix products. The affine offset of total-displacement Bloch BCs, u⁺ = u⁻ + (F̄ − I)·L, enters only the prescribed-value and residual side, not the tangent.
- The 4 unit-deformation problems χ^(ij) share the same reduced matrix: the converged tangent, with the same constraints. Solve them as one multi-column back-substitution `F \ B` with B of size n×4 against a single Cholesky factor. Behrou et al.'s condensation Ψ = −Ω Tₚ K⁻¹ Tₚᵀ is the same multiple-RHS pattern.
- Energy-based vs asymptotic homogenization (Xia & Breitkopf) mainly matters for code compactness and sensitivity form. The nonlinear case still needs the converged-state tangent and an adjoint (Q4).

### Gaps
- I could not access the Andreassen & Andreasen (2014) code text. I believe it maps periodic nodes through `edofMat`, fixes one node, and solves the three unit-strain cases with one backslash on a multi-column RHS, but this is [UNVERIFIED] in this session.
- I found no peer-reviewed quantitative cost comparison (time and conditioning) of master–slave vs Lagrange vs penalty specifically for TO unit cells; Henyš et al. (2019) may contain one, but I could not read it.

## Q4. Adjoint efficiency: one adjoint per scalar function; aggregating the adjoint right-hand sides with weights ∂J/∂𝔸 for an objective J(𝔸_M) in nonlinear homogenization

### Takeaway
Yes, it can be aggregated. The adjoint operator, the converged symmetric tangent, is the same for every component of 𝔸_M, and the adjoint equation is linear in its right-hand side. So for any scalar J(𝔸_M), one adjoint solve with RHS Σ_c (∂J/∂𝔸_c)·g_c gives exactly the same gradient as solving one adjoint per component and combining afterwards. The "one adjoint per scalar response" principle is the standard result of the adjoint method (Tortorelli & Michaleris 1994). Behrou et al. (2021) apply it directly to a criterion Z that is an explicit function of the homogenized tangent C_eff, computing one adjoint χ̃ per criterion. I did not find an explicit statement of the per-component-vs-aggregated equivalence in Wallin & Tortorelli (2020), whose full text I could not access, but it follows directly from linearity.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR] Tortorelli & Michaleris (1994)**, "Design sensitivity analysis: overview and review", *Inverse Probl. Eng.* 1(1):71–105, [doi:10.1080/174159794088027573](https://doi.org/10.1080/174159794088027573), and **Michaleris, Tortorelli & Vidal (1994)**, "Tangent operators and design sensitivity formulations for transient non-linear coupled problems with applications to elastoplasticity", *IJNME* 37:2471–2499, [doi:10.1002/nme.1620371408](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1620371408). These are the canonical references for direct vs adjoint sensitivity: the direct method costs one linear solve per design variable, the adjoint method one per response function. Bibliographic details were verified via Crossref; the text was not re-read in this session.
- **[PR] Behrou et al. (2021)** ([arXiv:2005.09111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09111), §3):
  - Eq. (23)–(24): dZ/dρ_e = ∂Z/∂ρ_e + χ̃ᵀ ∂r/∂ρ_e, with (∂r/∂s)ᵀ χ̃ = −(∂Z/∂s)ᵀ, "where Z is defined as the objective function or a constraint". [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09111)

### Inferences
Derivation in NEMOpt notation: reduced periodic DOFs, converged state u, density ρ̃.
- **State and χ problems.** R(u, ρ̃) = 0 with symmetric tangent K = ∂R/∂u. The unit-deformation problems are K χ_c = −b_c(u, ρ̃) for c = 1..4. The components are 𝔸_k = 𝔸_k(u, χ, ρ̃), and the objective is J = J(𝔸).
- **Lagrangian.** L = J + μᵀ R + Σ_c η_cᵀ (K χ_c + b_c).
- **χ-adjoints.** Stationarity in χ_c gives K η_c = −Σ_k (∂J/∂𝔸_k) ∂𝔸_k/∂χ_c. If 𝔸 is evaluated in the energy (variational) form, its stationarity in χ makes the RHS vanish at the solution, so η_c = 0. This is why linear homogenization TO needs no adjoint (Sigmund 1994; Xia & Breitkopf 2015). Even when it does not vanish, all η_c use the same factor of K.
- **u-adjoint.** Stationarity in u gives K μ = −[Σ_k w_k ∂𝔸_k/∂u + Σ_c ∂(K χ_c + b_c)/∂uᵀ η_c], with w_k = ∂J/∂𝔸_k. The ∂𝔸/∂u term contains the derivative of the tangent with respect to u (third derivative of the stored energy) contracted with χ.
- **Why aggregation is exact.** The right-hand side is linear in the weights w_k and the operator K does not depend on k. So one μ per scalar J replaces "one adjoint per needed 𝔸 component". The same holds for the filter chain rule: the PDE-filter back-substitution is linear, so apply it once to the aggregated dJ/dρ̃ rather than once per component.
- **Cost in NEMOpt.** With the converged-state factor reused (Q2), each extra adjoint is only a pair of triangular solves, O(nnz(L)). So the big win is factorization reuse; aggregation then reduces the adjoint stage from n_components solves plus n_components filter back-substitutions to 1 + 1, and simplifies code. Constraints that are explicit in ρ (e.g. volume) need no state adjoint.
- **Behavior preservation.** Aggregation changes only floating-point summation order: Σ_k w_k (μ_kᵀ ∂R/∂ρ) vs (Σ_k w_k μ_k)ᵀ ∂R/∂ρ. Gradients should agree to round-off, about 1e-12 relative. That is a suitable regression tolerance and a natural unit test: per-component vs aggregated gradient.

I found no paper that explicitly benchmarks per-component vs aggregated adjoints, likely because the equivalence is regarded as elementary.

## Q5. Newton strategies for nonlinear TO: load stepping, warm start, line search/arc length, adaptive steps, convergence criteria, robustness with distorted low-density elements, and the cost share of the nonlinear solve

### Takeaway
Published nonlinear TO codes use one or more of the following:
- full Newton with an Armijo line search (Senne et al.)
- arc-length continuation (Behrou et al.)
- adaptive or bisected load or strain increments (Thillaithevan et al.; Zhang et al.)
- stabilization of void-element distortion: energy interpolation, element removal, element connectivity parameterization (ECP), element deformation scaling, additive hyperelasticity

In the one geometric-nonlinear TO study that reports a breakdown, Newton plus objective evaluation dominates runtime (~75–77% of total time). Factorization-frequency control cut that by 30–69% with near-identical optima. I found no paper that quantifies warm-starting Newton from the previous design's converged state in a homogenization setting. Indirect evidence (≤3 Newton iterations per design in Senne et al.) suggests large savings, but it also changes which equilibrium branch is found when instabilities exist.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR] Senne, Gomes & Santos (2022)** ([arXiv:2112.09040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09040)), on 2D geometric-nonlinear compliance and mechanism problems (e.g. 400×100, 600×75 meshes):
  - Newton stopping criterion ‖r‖∞ ≤ 1e-5, with "Armijo's line search" for global convergence. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09040)
  - **Cost share:** "With Newton's method, the evaluation of the objective function takes 77.1% of the total time … for the cantilever beam, and 74.5% for the slender beam". [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09040)
  - "If Newton's method is employed, it takes at most 3 iterations to reach the approximate solution … the average time spent to compute the objective function is about 4.0 seconds". Modified Newton gives 2.5–3.0 s but sometimes slower convergence (10 iterations at SPLP iteration 210). [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09040)
  - "In the first five iterations of the SPLP method, Newton's method is always applied … to reduce the impact of the starting solution", which implies the starting solution carries over between designs. The exact initial-point rule was not in the text I read. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09040)
- **[PR] Behrou et al. (2021)** ([arXiv:2005.09111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09111)): "At each design iteration, the microscale nonlinear finite element problem is solved using the arc-length method", which they say is efficient for problems with "one or more critical instabilities". Convergence: "relative change of the residuals … less than 10⁻¹⁰". [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09111)
- **[PR] Thillaithevan et al. (2024)** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12638428/)): the Newton–Raphson unit-cell solver first attempts the target strain. If that fails, it solves at a point "halfway between the current target strain" and the last converged one, repeating until convergence. This is adaptive bisection load stepping. Energy interpolation was not used but "can be included". [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12638428/)
- **[PR] Zhang, Khandelwal & Guo (2023)**, "Finite strain topology optimization with nonlinear stability constraints", *CMAME* 413:116119, [doi:10.1016/j.cma.2023.116119](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2023.116119) ([arXiv:2303.14911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14911)):
  - They list the remedies for "mesh distortions of void (or low-density) elements": neglecting void DOFs in the NR convergence check, element removal/reintroduction, zero-length links (ECP), distortion-indicator design updates, and linear energy interpolation. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14911)
  - They use adaptive linear energy interpolation, F = I + η(ρ)∇u with β = 120 and c = 0.08, where c is increased by 0.05 "if FEA fails to converge after reaching a predefined minimum step size" and reset after convergence. They call this adaptivity "crucial". [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14911)
  - NR uses "an adaptive step-size strategy", and convergence is declared "when the energy residual drops below 1.0E-12". [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14911)
- **[PR] Wang, Lazarov, Sigmund & Jensen (2014)**, "Interpolation scheme for fictitious domain techniques and topology optimization of finite strain elastic problems", *CMAME* 276:453–472, [doi:10.1016/j.cma.2014.03.021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2014.03.021). "Numerical instability … can often be observed, due to excessive distortion in low stiffness regions". Their energy interpolation uses large-deformation theory for solid and small-deformation theory for void (abstract-level via search).
- **[PR] Other robustness references** (bibliographic details verified via Crossref; content not re-read here):
  - Bruns & Tortorelli (2003), "An element removal and reintroduction strategy for the topology optimization of structures and compliant mechanisms", *IJNME* 57:1413–1430, [doi:10.1002/nme.783](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.783).
  - Yoon & Kim (2005), "Element connectivity parameterization for topology optimization of geometrically nonlinear structures", *Int. J. Solids Struct.* 42:1983–2009, [doi:10.1016/j.ijsolstr.2004.09.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijsolstr.2004.09.005).

  - Wallin, Ivarsson & Tortorelli (2018), "Stiffness optimization of non-linear elastic structures", *CMAME* 330:292–307, [doi:10.1016/j.cma.2017.11.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2017.11.004).
- **[PR] Other cost-share data points** (all linear or mixed contexts):
  - Lazarov et al. (2016): state solves can exceed 99% of runtime in large linear TO ([ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/140201797/main.pdf)).
  - Ferrari & Sigmund (2020): 85–96% after optimizing everything else ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05436)).
  - Träff (2023): a matrix-free nonlinear operator application costs 1075.4 ms vs 1.316 ms for the linear one (2·10⁶ elements, A100 GPU), because nonlinear "local matrices are numerically integrated for every matrix-vector product". He used "a Newton-Krylov method with a backtracking line-search" ([ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/338184592/Erik_Albert_Tr_ff.pdf)).

### Inferences
- The unit-cell problem is hyperelastic, so path-independent. The 10 fixed load steps from zero only serve Newton robustness. Three cheaper strategies are literature-supported:
  - (i) Adaptive stepping: attempt the full load, bisect on failure (Thillaithevan et al.; Zhang et al.).
  - (ii) Warm start: initialize from the previous design's converged fluctuation field, where Senne et al.'s ≤3 iterations per design is suggestive.
  - (iii) Factorization-frequency control: modified or inexact Newton (Senne et al.).
- Behavior-preservation risk. (ii) and (iii) can change results. With buckling-prone microstructures a warm start can converge to a different equilibrium branch than the zero-start path. Inexact strategies change round-off and convergence paths, and Senne et al. report only "very similar" optima, not identical ones. For a refactor that must reproduce the article, keep "10 steps from zero, full Newton" as the reference mode. Add warm start and adaptive steps as opt-in modes, validated against reference designs, with automatic fallback to the reference stepping when Newton fails.
- Constitutive-call reduction (87 per Gauss point today): evaluate stress and tangent together in one call per Newton iteration. Cache the converged-state stress, tangent and, if needed, the third-derivative contraction for the χ and adjoint stages instead of re-evaluating them per χ and per adjoint. With a fixed 10-step schedule, the floor is roughly 10 × (Newton iterations per step) + 1 evaluations per Gauss point.
- Convergence criteria in the literature vary: relative residual change < 1e-10 (Behrou), ‖r‖∞ ≤ 1e-5 (Senne), energy residual < 1e-12 (Zhang). For an adjoint-consistent gradient, a tight tolerance (relative residual about 1e-10 or energy-norm based) is preferable, because gradient error scales with the residual at "convergence".

### Gaps
- I found no paper that quantifies warm-start savings for nonlinear homogenization TO, or that reports the cost share of the unit-cell Newton solve vs χ vs adjoint stages in a homogenization pipeline.
- I could not access Buhl et al. (2000), Bruns & Tortorelli (2003), Luo et al. (2015) or van Dijk et al. (2014) full texts to quote their Newton or convergence settings.

## Q6. PDE (Helmholtz) filter efficiency: constant matrix, factor once, periodic BCs, cost relative to density filters

### Takeaway
The Helmholtz PDE filter matrix depends only on the mesh and the filter length, not on the design. It should be assembled and factored once per optimization run and then applied by back-substitution: one forward filter per design, and one transpose application per scalar sensitivity, or one in total with aggregated sensitivities. top88's PDE variant does exactly this (`LF = chol(KF,'lower')` outside the loop). Its advantages over classical density filters are memory and scaling with filter radius, especially in 3D and with iterative or multigrid solvers. In small 2D meshes with direct solves it can be slower per iteration than convolution filters.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR] Lazarov & Sigmund (2011, online 2010)**, "Filters in topology optimization based on Helmholtz-type differential equations", *IJNME* 86:765–781, [doi:10.1002/nme.3072](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.3072). The publisher page returned 403; content is taken from the two papers below. Per a secondary source, the PDE length parameter relates to the density-filter radius as r = R/(2√3) ([Lazarov et al. 2016, Eq. 10](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/140201797/main.pdf)).
- **[PR] top88 (2011)** ([Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946)):
  - The PDE variant (top82) assembles `KF` once, `LF = chol(KF,'lower')` once, and builds `TF` once before the loop. "In order to keep the MATLAB code readable, the linear system … is solved by factorization instead of an iterative method, which hides some of the filter advantages." [Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946)
  - The classical filter's "computational complexity and the memory utilization are proportional to r_min² in two dimensions and to r_min³ in three dimensions". The PDE filter "utilizes the mesh used for the state problem … which avoids excessive memory usage", and with an iterative solver its "cost depends linearly on the length parameter". "For a large filter radius, especially in three dimensions, the PDE filtering scheme should be the preferred choice." [Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946)
  - Timings in seconds per iteration on MBB meshes 60×20 / 150×50 / 300×100:

    | Filtering | 88-line | conv2 | PDE |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Sensitivity filtering | 0.15 / 0.72 / 1.98 | 0.13 / 0.69 / 1.85 | 0.13 / 0.78 / 2.18 |
    | Density filtering | 0.12 / 0.94 / 5.67 | 0.16 / 0.78 / 3.30 | 0.19 / 1.79 / 10.08 |

    The density-filter PDE variant is slower because the filter is re-applied "in every iteration of the bisection algorithm", involving "a relatively costly backsubstitution". This "could be avoided" because the PDE filter is volume-preserving. [Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946)
- **[PR, review] Lazarov, Wang & Sigmund (2016)** ([ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/140201797/main.pdf)):
  - The PDE filter is "volume preserving for Neumann boundary conditions". Its advantages are "lower computational cost, the re-utilization of solvers developed for the state problems, relatively easy parallelization for large scale problems", and spatially varying filter parameters. [ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/140201797/main.pdf)
  - "The computational cost of the PDE filter using multigrid solvers is O(n)." [ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/140201797/main.pdf)
- **[PR] Ferrari & Sigmund (2020)** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05436)): on regular grids they implement the density filter with `imfilter` ("essentially as efficient as conv2"). Filter BCs (Neumann "symmetric" vs zero-Dirichlet) can lead to different local minima. Filtering plus update took 0.4% of a 3D run. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05436)

### Inferences
- Re-assembling the filter matrix on every call, as NEMOpt does now, has no literature justification: the matrix is design-independent. Assemble it once, factor it once with Cholesky, since the Helmholtz operator with periodic tying is SPD, and store the factor in the problem setup.
- Periodic BCs on the filter go through the same master–slave DOF mapping as the mechanics, on the node-based scalar field. They also preserve volume, because there is no boundary flux on a periodic cell. This is my inference from the Neumann volume-preservation statement plus periodicity.
- On a structured periodic grid, the PDE filter could alternatively be applied by FFT. That would change results only at round-off, but it is not needed at NEMOpt's size, since one back-substitution on a ~1.6·10³–6.4·10³-node scalar problem is cheap.

### Gaps
- The full text of Lazarov & Sigmund (2011) was not accessible, so I have no direct quotes on periodic BCs or their own timing comparisons.

## Q7. Structured-grid tricks: precomputed element geometric quantities, element-by-element/matrix-free operators, multithreaded assembly with mesh coloring

### Takeaway
On a uniform structured grid, all elements share the same reference-to-physical mapping. Shape-function gradients, Jacobian determinants and quadrature weights can therefore be computed once and reused. In linear TO this extends to a single precomputed element stiffness scaled by density, which is the basis of top88/top99neo and of matrix-free GPU codes. In nonlinear elasticity the tangent depends on the local deformation, so constitutive evaluation and integration must be redone per element per Newton iteration. The geometric precomputation still applies, but the "single Ke" trick does not. Threaded assembly with mesh coloring or atomics scales well in Ferrite (about 7–8× on 8 tasks), and the per-element constitutive work in nonlinear problems is embarrassingly parallel.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR] top88 / top99neo** use a single precomputed `KE`/`Ke` for all elements, with assembly as `sK = KE(:)*(Emin+xPhys.^penal*(E0-Emin))`-style scaling ([Lirias preprint](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/225946); [arXiv:2005.05436](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05436)).
- **[Thesis] Träff (2023)** ([ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/338184592/Erik_Albert_Tr_ff.pdf)):
  - Linear case: "the local element matrices are all identical to some scaling, since all elements have identical geometry. This allows for one single element matrix to be integrated offline, and reused … circumventing the need for numerical integration when applying the stiffness matrix". [ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/338184592/Erik_Albert_Tr_ff.pdf)
  - Nonlinear case: element matrices are "no longer identical save a scaling, as they were now strain dependent", and "nonlinear problems are not able to achieve the same computational improvement by using a Cartesian grid, as individual elements require numerical integration at every tangent evaluation regardless of the identical element shapes". Storing the matrix or stencil on all multigrid levels "comes with an increase in memory requirements of nearly a factor 100". [ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/338184592/Erik_Albert_Tr_ff.pdf)
  - The nonlinear matrix-free operator application is about 800× more expensive than the linear one (1075.4 ms vs 1.316 ms). To avoid data races he used graph coloring (citing Zegard & Paulino) or node-wise updates. [ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/338184592/Erik_Albert_Tr_ff.pdf)
- **[DOC] Ferrite.jl threaded assembly** ([how-to](https://ferrite-fem.github.io/Ferrite.jl/stable/howto/threaded_assembly/)):
  - Coloring ensures that "within each color, no two elements share degrees of freedom, we can safely assemble each color in parallel". The workstream coloring algorithm is the default ("more balanced colors"); greedy uses fewer colors on structured grids. [Ferrite how-to](https://ferrite-fem.github.io/Ferrite.jl/stable/howto/threaded_assembly/)
  - Each task needs its own `ScratchData` (cell cache, a copy of `CellValues`, local buffers, assembler with `fillzero=false`). [Ferrite how-to](https://ferrite-fem.github.io/Ferrite.jl/stable/howto/threaded_assembly/)
  - Timings on a 10-core machine: colored 0.82 s (1 task) → 0.11 s (8 tasks); atomic 0.73 s → 0.10 s. Coloring itself costs about 0.3 s, once. "The atomic version is even a bit faster than the colored version." [Ferrite how-to](https://ferrite-fem.github.io/Ferrite.jl/stable/howto/threaded_assembly/)
- **[PR] Engblom & Lukarski (2016):** parallel triplet compression gives 4–10× on 6–16 cores ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.1066)). This applies if NEMOpt keeps a COO path.
- **[PR] Lazarov et al. (2016) / Amir et al. (2014) / Aage et al. (2015, 2017):** structured-grid multigrid and parallel frameworks are the route to 10⁶–10⁹ elements ([ORBIT PDF](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/140201797/main.pdf); [doi:10.1038/nature23911](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature23911)).

### Inferences
- For NEMOpt's uniform quad grid, precompute once: ∂N/∂X at each Gauss point, det J, and the weights, for 4 Gauss points per element (or however many the code uses). This removes per-element Jacobian work. The neo-Hookean stress and tangent evaluation, 87 calls per Gauss point today, remains the dominant per-element cost. Reducing the call count (Q5) matters more than the geometric precomputation.
- Threading the element loop (constitutive evaluation plus local Ke and fe) with per-thread buffers and coloring or atomics is the natural next step after serial optimizations. At 40×40 to 80×80 the absolute gains are modest. Threads must not change results: coloring gives a deterministic summation order, while atomics do not. This matters for bitwise regression tests.
- Matrix-free operators are not attractive for NEMOpt. Träff shows that nonlinear matrix-free application costs about 800× the linear one, and direct factorization is cheap at this size.

### Gaps
- I did not verify Wu, Dick & Westermann (2016) or Schmidt & Schulz (2011) GPU papers directly. Their specific speedups for identical-element stencils are [UNVERIFIED].
- I found no Julia-specific study of the speedup from precomputed geometric quantities in nonlinear TO; NEMOpt would need to benchmark it.
